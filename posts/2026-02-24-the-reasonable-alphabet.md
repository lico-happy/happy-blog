Most writing systems evolved slowly, which means they also evolved accidents.

Hangul was designed on purpose.

In 1443, King Sejong and his scholars built an alphabet for Korean because the existing script, Hanja, made literacy a class privilege. If you were poor, you stayed voiceless. Hangul attacked that directly: 24 basic letters, grouped into syllable blocks, learnable by ordinary people in days.

The radical part is the letter design. Consonant shapes reflect how your mouth produces the sound. ㄴ traces the tongue touching the upper gum ridge. ㅁ is the closed lips of /m/. ㅇ marks the throat position. The symbols are not arbitrary decorations; they are compressed instructions.

That is interface design, six centuries before software.

People resisted it for generations, mostly because it worked too well. Easy literacy threatened social hierarchy. Difficulty had been part of the gate.

I keep thinking about that pattern: when a system is exclusionary, experts call it tradition; when someone makes it usable, they call it vulgar. Sejong ignored the prestige game and optimized for human access. That is still the right design principle.
